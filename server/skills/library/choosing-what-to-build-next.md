# Choosing what to build next

This company generates far more good ideas than it can build. That is not a
problem to solve; it is the permanent condition. The skill is not producing
options, it is discarding them quickly and without regret.

## The question that orders the list

Not "what is most valuable" — almost everything on the list is valuable, which
is why it is on the list. Ask instead:

> **What do we not know, that we would find out fastest by building this?**

A company with no customers does not have a prioritization problem, it has an
ignorance problem: it does not know what people will pay for, whether the
thing works, or whether anyone will answer an email. Every hour should be
buying an answer.

This reorders a list dramatically. The large obviously-valuable feature often
teaches you nothing you didn't already believe. The small awkward one — put a
price on the page, email ten people, ship the ugly version — is where the
information is.

## Three practical tests

**The reversibility test.** Can this be undone cheaply? Reversible decisions
should be made fast and alone; irreversible ones deserve the slow
conversation. Most people spend their care backwards, deliberating over things
they could simply try.

**The blocking test.** Is anything else waiting on this? A small task that
unblocks three others outranks a large one that unblocks nothing, and this is
the one place where "it's quick" is a real argument.

**The evidence test.** What is the case for this? Rank it honestly:

1. A customer asked for it, in their own words.
2. Something observed — a number, a failure, a reply.
3. A plausible inference from something observed.
4. It seems obviously right.

Most of what any team builds sits at 4 and is described as though it were at
1. When the whole list is at 4, the real next task is to go get some evidence:
`check_replies`, `check_service`, or an email to ten people.

## What to do with the rest

Write it down and stop thinking about it. `queue_work` and `log_venture_note`
exist so an idea can be set aside without being lost — and an idea that is
safely recorded stops occupying the argument.

Do not build "while we're in there". A change that was not worth doing on its
own does not become worth doing because a file is already open; it becomes a
change nobody decided to make, in a diff nobody expected it in.

## Saying no properly

A rejected idea deserves a reason, and the reason is almost never "that's a
bad idea". It is usually one of:

- *Not yet* — it depends on something we haven't learned.
- *Not this* — something else buys the same answer cheaper.
- *Not us* — real, but not what this venture is.

Say which. "Not yet" and "not us" lead to completely different follow-ups, and
collapsing both into silence is how the same idea comes back every week.

## When the founder has decided

If the founder has picked, that is the answer, and relitigating it costs a
turn and some trust. State a concern once, plainly, and then do the work —
properly, not grudgingly. A concern raised once and set aside is a
contribution; the same concern raised three times is an obstruction.
