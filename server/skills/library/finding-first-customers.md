# Finding the first customers

The first ten are not a smaller version of the first thousand. They come from
individually identified people you contacted on purpose, and none of the
mechanisms that work later — content, SEO, ads, word of mouth — work at all at
this size, because they all need an audience the venture does not have yet.

This is the step most often replaced with something that feels like progress:
building more of the product, writing a launch post, setting up analytics for
traffic that does not exist. Each is measurable and none of them produces a
customer.

## Find the place the problem is already being discussed

The first ten are people complaining about the problem in public, today. So
the search is for the complaint, not the product.

For a developer API: the GitHub issues of adjacent libraries, Stack Overflow
questions with no good answer, the subreddit where people post the workaround
they built themselves. Someone who has already written a hacky script to do
what you are selling is the highest-intent prospect that exists — they have
proven the problem is worth effort, and your pitch is "you can delete that
script."

Be specific enough to name individuals. "Developers who build travel apps" is
not a list. "The eleven people who opened issues about timezone handling in
these three repos in the last six months" is a list, and it is a better one
than any bought database.

## Go where a stranger is allowed to talk to you

Rank channels by whether an unsolicited message is *welcome* there:

1. **A warm introduction.** Anyone who will forward your email with a sentence
   of their own attached. Converts several times better than anything below.
2. **A public request for help you can actually answer.** Answer it properly,
   in public, without pitching. Mention the product only if it is the answer.
   This is slow per contact and produces the best-qualified conversations.
3. **A cold email to a named person about their specific situation.** Works,
   at a low rate, if it is genuinely specific. See `customer-outreach` before
   sending one — it reaches a real human and cannot be recalled.
4. **A launch post** (Show HN, a directory, Product Hunt). One spike of
   traffic, almost no retention, and not repeatable. Fine as an event; it is
   not a channel, and treating it as one is why ventures stall after it.

Do one channel properly before adding a second. Ten contacts through one
channel tells you whether the channel works. Two contacts through five
channels tells you nothing about any of them.

## Tell interest from politeness

People are kind, especially to someone who built something. Compliments are
not data. Only these count:

- they used it, more than once, unprompted
- they paid, or asked what it costs before you said
- they introduced you to someone else with the same problem
- they complained about something specific and asked when it would be fixed

"This is cool", "definitely useful", "send me the link" and "let's talk next
quarter" are all the same answer, and that answer is no. A second use is worth
more than any amount of enthusiasm, because it is the only signal the person
was not being nice.

Record what actually happened rather than how the conversation felt. Contact
memory exists so the next attempt starts from "this person used it twice and
went quiet" instead of from nothing.

## Ask about the past, not the future

"Would you pay for this?" invites a guess about a hypothetical self, and the
guess is always more generous than the person. Ask what they did:

- What did you do last time this came up?
- What did that cost you — hours, money, an outage?
- What are you using now, and what is wrong with it?
- Who else on your team hits this?

A person who has no answer to the first question does not have the problem,
whatever they say about the product. That is the finding, and it is worth more
than a polite yes, because it means the next ten contacts should look
different.

## Ten conversations is the deliverable

The first milestone is not revenue. It is ten real conversations with named
people who have the problem, and an honest note on each. Report the count and
what was learned, not the effort.

If ten conversations produce no second use and no one asking the price, the
finding is about the venture, not about the outreach — and saying so early is
worth more to the company than another week of polish. Nothing in the
company's incentives rewards keeping a dead venture alive; the profit share
pays for revenue and shipped work, and a venture that nobody wants produces
neither.
