## Q1. What does an early-stage diligence process actually consist of, step by step? What artifacts are produced?

### Takeaway
Early-stage (pre-seed/seed) diligence is deliberately thin and hypothesis-confirming rather than exhaustive: a few days to two weeks of work whose purpose is to check that the two or three signals that created initial interest are real, with the decisive work being back-channel reference calls and a written investment memo. The heavier apparatus — market maps, 20–40 page thesis decks, expert-call programmes — belongs to *thesis* work done ahead of any specific deal, or to Series A.

### Cited Findings
- Early-stage diligence is framed as "confirming the few signals that sparked investor interest and checking that those signals are real and repeatable"; at pre-seed/seed "diligence must be fast, focused, and right-sized — Series A-style data requests overwhelm early founders." — [VC Lab, "How VCs Do Early Stage Diligence in Emerging VC" (Nov 2025)](https://govclab.com/2025/11/05/early-stage-diligence-in-emerging-vc/)
- "For pre-seed and seed rounds, diligence takes days to two weeks and is light; the bet is on the people." — [VC Lab / Early-Stage Diligence: Building VC Conviction Fast](https://govclab.com/2025/11/05/early-stage-diligence-in-emerging-vc/)
- At seed stage, diligence is described as an 80/20 exercise: "just a couple phone calls and research work" let a VC learn quickly whether the initial hypothesis was correct, via strong customer calls and back-channel personal references. — [NextView Ventures, "VC's Doing the Work to Build Investment Conviction"](https://nextview.vc/blog/vcs-doing-the-work-to-build-investment-conviction/)
- The standard deal-team artifact chain: completed questionnaire + requested documents → findings summarised in a **deal qualification memorandum** → that memo is "typically the trigger point for the VC firm to talk to the startup about deal terms and present a term sheet." — [Affinity, Due Diligence Checklist for Venture Capital](https://www.affinity.co/guides/due-diligence-checklist-for-venture-capital)
- A founder-survey/questionnaire is used as input to "a couple hour session with the founders," which then "feed[s] into the investment memo process." — [Angel Capital Association, Due Diligence Playbook (PDF)](https://www.angelcapitalassociation.org/data/Documents/Members%20Only/BestPractices/E3e%20-%20Due%20Diligence%20Checklists%20and%20Reports/Due_Diligence_Playbook_Generic_with_Appendices.pdf)
- **Thesis-stage artifact set (pre-deal).** Alpaca VC's "Field Study" programme: investors "spend three to four months speaking with investors, founders, customers and incumbents in that space"; the output is "an extensive deck (20–40+ pages), market map, and deep analysis outlining market insights and findings about investable opportunities." MBA associates are explicitly staffed onto at least one Field Study during their tenure. — [Alpaca VC, "Alpaca's Field Study Program"](https://medium.com/alpaca-vc/alpaca-vcs-field-study-program-a-repeatable-thesis-driven-approach-to-investment-5b1096108ff1)
- A market map is judged by construction method, not company count: it requires "a business-description-based search methodology, coverage of long-tail private operators, verified ownership data, scale proxies, timing signals, and a connection to an active sourcing workflow." A complete map "becomes part of the investment thesis, explaining why a particular segment, company, or model makes sense now." — [Grata, "What a Complete Market Map Actually Requires"](https://grata.com/resources/what-a-complete-market-map-actually-requires)
- Pre-seed/seed diligence checklists in circulation are document-light (corporate/cap table/IP hygiene plus team and traction), and explicitly distinguished from Series B+ lists. — [Kruze Consulting, "VC Due Diligence Checklist: Pre-Seed to Series B & Beyond"](https://kruzeconsulting.com/blog/due-diligence-checklist/); [Chudson, "Due Diligence Checklist for Pre-Seed Companies"](https://chudson.substack.com/p/due-diligence-checklist-for-pre-seed)
- **2026 state of practice — AI in the loop.** Memo generation has "moved from experiment to workflow" for many deal teams: an agent ingests deal documents (CIM, model, call transcripts, market research), runs structured extraction across them in parallel, links each extracted data point back to source document and page number, and produces a uniform memo structure across the pipeline. — [Plausity, "AI x Private Equity 2026"](https://plausity.com/en/news/ai-private-equity-2026-repeatable-diligence-systems); [Third Bridge, "PE due diligence with AI: the complete workflow (2026)"](https://www.thirdbridge.com/en-us/about-us/media/perspectives/ai-due-diligence-private-equity)
- The claimed division of labour for agentic diligence: "deal screening, diligence synthesis, portfolio monitoring, IC memo prep, LP reporting, and operating-partner support," with the pitch that a solo GP can operate "with the leverage of a ten-person fund." — [VC Lab, "Agentic VC" (Apr 2026)](https://govclab.com/2026/04/21/agentic-vc-leading-the-ai-revolution/)

### Inferences
- The canonical artifact list an AI idea-generation team should be benchmarked against is roughly: (1) a **thesis/market map** built before any specific idea, (2) an **opportunity/investment memo** in a fixed template, (3) **primary-call notes** (customer, expert, back-channel reference), (4) a **competitive teardown**, and (5) an explicit **decision record** with a verdict. Everything else (data-room review, legal/IP, financial audit) is stage-inappropriate at idea stage.
- Note the asymmetry the sources reveal: the *time-expensive* research (3–4 months, 20–40 page deck) happens at **thesis** level and is amortised across many deals; the *per-idea* research is days. An AI team that spends weeks per idea is mis-allocating relative to human practice; an AI team that produces a 40-page deck per idea is producing a thesis artifact where a memo is called for.
- The 2026 AI-diligence tooling convention worth copying is **per-claim source-and-page provenance** as the hallucination control, and **uniform memo structure across the pipeline** so ideas are comparable.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve first-party, named-firm process descriptions from a16z, Sequoia, Bessemer, First Round Review or NfX within the tool budget (those domains were either not returned or blocked). The step-by-step here is assembled from VC Lab, Affinity, ACA and Alpaca, which are credible but not the marquee sources requested.
- No reliable figure found for what fraction of screened deals reach memo stage at a typical seed fund.

## Q2. How is market sizing done properly? Top-down TAM vs bottom-up; what makes a sizing credible to an IC; common fallacies.

### Takeaway
The consensus in current practitioner writing is that a top-down TAM alone is a credibility liability, and that the defensible construction is bottom-up: **(number of identifiable accounts in a precisely defined ICP) × (realistic ACV)**, built from named segments, with the top-down number used only as a sanity ceiling. The failure modes are the "1% fallacy," Gartner/IDC-report laundering, and optimising the TAM number for emotional effect rather than as a bound on the thesis.

### Cited Findings
- **The mechanics of bottom-up.** "Segmentation + customer counts + ARPU are the core building blocks of defensible market forecasts"; bottom-up = number of potential customers × price, and it "forces you to define who exactly your customer is, how many of them exist, and how much they might pay." — [Qubit Capital, "Bottom-Up Market Sizing for Startups"](https://qubit.capital/blog/bottom-up-market-sizing)
- **Why ICs prefer it.** "VCs actively distrust top-down-only decks because bottom-up forces you to defend price times ICP volume — the math investors run silently while you talk." — [UnicornScreener, "Total Addressable Market Sizing: The Framework VCs Actually Use"](https://unicornscreener.vc/blog/total-addressable-market-sizing-the-framework-vcs-actually-u)
- **The cross-check convention.** Reported 2026 practice: build top-down TAM from Gartner/IDC, then validate with bottom-up customer counting for SAM and SOM; "if your top-down SAM and bottom-up SOM are within ~20% of each other (after normalizing for the layer comparison), most 2026 VCs treat that as a credible market." *(Caveat: this is an SEO content page, not a named practitioner; the ~20% threshold should be treated as a rule of thumb in circulation, not a documented IC standard.)* — [ICanPitch, "TAM SAM SOM: Complete Market Sizing Guide for Startups (2026)"](https://www.icanpitch.com/blog/tam-sam-som-market-sizing-guide)
- **What makes a sizing credible.** "Methodology transparency, honest assumptions, and a clear narrative connecting TAM to your specific SOM to your actual revenue plan"; internal data (beta users, waitlist signups) materially strengthens the bottom-up. — [Waveup, "Top-Down vs Bottom-Up Market Sizing"](https://waveup.com/blog/top-down-and-bottom-up-market-size-calculation/); [Equidam, "How to Benchmark Your TAM and Build Investor Confidence"](https://www.equidam.com/benchmark-tam-startup-fundraising/)
- **The original, disciplined use of TAM — and how it degraded.** TAM was "originally a top-down sanity check": the VC asks how big the company could get capturing the whole market, applies a realistic capture rate of **1–10%** to model outcomes, and uses TAM "as a ceiling to bound the investment thesis, not inflate it." The degradation: "investors respond emotionally to big numbers — a $50 billion TAM produces one kind of Series B check, while a $500 billion TAM produces a much larger one." — [AGI Roundtable, "Total Addressable Market (TAM) Bullshit — A Primer in Market Manipulation"](https://agiroundtable.substack.com/p/total-addressable-market-tam-bullshit)
- **The 1% fallacy.** Founders "fail to distinguish between the dream and the deliverable," assuming they can capture a small percentage of a huge market "without explaining how." — [The Venture Crew, "Is the '1% Market Fallacy' Holding Your Startup Back?"](https://theventurecrew.substack.com/p/is-the-1-market-fallacy-holding-your)
- **The counter-critique (TAM under-rates good seed ideas).** "TAM is not as important as how you think about or estimate TAM"; a common VC fallacy at seed is that "a startup gets prematurely filtered out simply because the immediately visible market doesn't seem large enough." — [Blume Ventures, "TAM: Notes & Thoughts"](https://blume.vc/commentaries/tam-notes-thoughts); [An Operator's Blog, "The TAM Fallacy At Seed Stage"](https://operator.blog/2024/03/08/the-tam-fallacy-at-seed-stage/)
- **Sourcing hygiene.** "If your TAM slide is backed by flimsy sources or unexplained assumptions, it hurts your credibility." — [Entreprenerds, "Market Sizing: Why Most Founders Get TAM, SAM, and SOM Dangerously Wrong"](https://www.entreprenerds.uk/blog/market-sizing-why-most-founders-get-tam-sam-and-som-dangerously-wrong)
- **Studio practice mirrors this.** Hexa's Opportunity Memo instructs sizing be done "ideally by confronting bottom-up and top-down analysis" — i.e. the two methods are treated as a triangulation pair, not alternatives. — [Hexa Opportunity Memo Template (via inniches.com)](https://inniches.com/opportunity-memo-template-quentin-nickmans-startup-studio-hexa)

### Inferences
- The operationally useful definition of "credible sizing" for a EUR 1M-ARR-target software/AI/API business is not a TAM at all — it is a **SOM construction**: a countable list of target accounts (ideally enumerable from a real source: a company database, an app-store/marketplace listing, a job-posting count, a public registry), times a defended ACV, times a defended win-rate, reconciled to a revenue plan. At EUR 1M ARR, the arithmetic is small enough to be fully enumerable (e.g. 100 accounts at EUR 10k, or 1,000 at EUR 1k), which means an AI team has no excuse for a top-down number.
- The sizing fallacy list worth encoding as automated checks: (a) top-down-only, (b) unexplained capture rate ("we only need 1%"), (c) analyst-report laundering with no primary anchor, (d) ACV assumed rather than derived from observed competitor pricing, (e) conflating "people who have the problem" with "people who have a budget line and a buyer," (f) counting a market that a competitor's pricing page shows is already being served at 1/10th the assumed ACV.
- Blume's and the Operator's Blog critique cuts the other way and should be represented: a sizing gate that rejects small visible markets will reject exactly the category-creating ideas. The right IC standard is *how the estimate was built*, not the magnitude.

### Gaps
- I did not find a first-party a16z/Bessemer/Sequoia essay on sizing methodology within budget. The best-known TAM critiques in circulation (e.g. Bill Gurley on TAM, Tomasz Tunguz) did not surface; the writer should not attribute the critiques above to those authors.
- No source gave a documented, named-fund IC rubric (i.e. an actual scorecard) for sizing. The "~20%" convergence rule is unverified.

## Q3. How much research is proportionate at idea stage vs seed vs Series A? Hours, and what is deliberately NOT researched.

### Takeaway
Reported 2026 norms: pre-seed/seed diligence runs **days to ~2–4 weeks**; Series A runs **4–8 weeks**; post-term-sheet confirmatory diligence is ~30 days. At the earliest stage the deliberate omissions are financial audit, detailed unit-economics modelling, IP/legal deep-dives and cohort analysis — because there is no data — and the substitution is people-diligence plus a small number of high-signal primary calls.

### Cited Findings
- Seed diligence: "two to four weeks, with standard deals expecting 2–3 weeks, up to 4 weeks for complex situations." Pre-seed: "30 days of confirmatory diligence... though SAFE rounds are typically 1 to 2 weeks between commitment and wire." — [SheetVenture, "How Long Does VC Diligence Take?"](https://sheetventure.com/fundraising-knowledge/how-long-does-vc-due-diligence-usually-take); [Capwave, "How long does fundraising take? pre-seed and seed in 2026"](https://capwave.ai/blog/blog-how-long-does-fundraising-take-2026)
- Series A: "four to eight weeks of diligence"; "significantly more sophisticated than at seed, expecting 4–6 weeks of back-and-forth even with cooperative investors." — [Pitchwise, "How Long Does It Take to Raise a Series A in 2026?"](https://www.pitchwise.se/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-raise-a-series-a-in-2026)
- Full-round context (2026): pre-seed/seed closes in **12–16 weeks** from first investor meeting to wire; Series A = 4–8 weeks preparation + 6–10 weeks active pitching + 4–8 weeks diligence + 3–6 weeks to close. — [Capwave (2026)](https://capwave.ai/blog/blog-how-long-does-fundraising-take-2026); [Pitchwise (2026)](https://www.pitchwise.se/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-raise-a-series-a-in-2026)
- **Hours, partner-level.** One figure in circulation: "partners make around 10 reference calls across approximately 118 hours of diligence." *(I could not fetch the underlying NextView page to confirm context — treat as unverified, and note it likely describes a lead investor's total across a full deal, not an idea-stage screen.)* — [NextView Ventures](https://nextview.vc/blog/vcs-doing-the-work-to-build-investment-conviction/), as reported via search; [hub.causo.ai, "VC reference calls: what investors really ask (2026)"](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/vc-reference-calls)
- **Angel-side effort/return correlation (older, frequently cited).** "Angel investors dedicating over 40 hours to due diligence achieve 7.1x returns versus 1.1x for those spending less than 20 hours." *(This is the Wiltbank/Kauffman "Returns to Angel Investors in Groups" finding from 2007, recirculated; it is nearly two decades old and applies to angels, not institutional seed funds — the writer should date it clearly.)* — recirculated in [Qubit Capital, VC Due Diligence Checklist](https://qubit.capital/blog/vc-due-diligence-checklist)
- **What is substituted at the earliest stage.** "Much of pre-seed and seed stage investing is about the people versus the product, so investors drill in on what makes the founding team want to do this and what they view as success." — [StartUpNV, "A Pre-Seed Due Diligence Checklist for Founders"](https://startupnv.org/understand-investor-expectations-a-pre-seed-due-diligence-checklist-for-founders/)
- **Studio per-idea budget, for contrast.** PSL "spends roughly four months working hard to quickly validate" an idea — prototypes, customer conversations, market analysis — which is far more than a VC spends per deal, because the studio is also doing the founding work, not just the deciding. — [PSL Studio](https://www.psl.com/studio); [InnoLead, Venture Studio Profile: Pioneer Square Labs](https://www.innovationleader.com/uncategorized/venture-studio-profile-pioneer-square-labs/)
- High Alpha's per-idea commitment at the decision point is compressed to a single week ("Sprint Week"), following an open-ended "Exploration" phase. — [High Alpha, Sprint Week](https://highalpha.com/sprint-week/)

### Inferences
- The right benchmark ladder for an AI idea team, reading across the sources: **idea screen = hours** (kill most here); **opportunity memo = 1–5 days**; **validation sprint = 1–4 weeks** (studio-style, with a build/test artifact); **investable conviction = 1–4 months** (PSL's four months, Alpaca's 3–4 month thesis). An AI team's comparative advantage is at the first two rungs, where the human cost of screening is what forces the funnel narrow.
- The deliberate non-research list at idea stage is as informative as the research list: no financial model beyond a one-page ACV × accounts arithmetic, no legal/IP, no security/compliance review, no detailed CAC/LTV (no data exists), no org design, no exit-comparables analysis. Anything requiring a real customer base is deferred.
- The 40-hour angel finding is frequently used to argue "more diligence is better." It should be quoted with its vintage and population; it does not establish that more desk research helps at idea stage — the Wiltbank work attributes returns to *primary* diligence (meeting people), which aligns with Q4.

### Gaps
- No source gave a defensible figure for **associate-hours per screened deal** at a named seed fund. The "118 hours" number is the closest and is unverified.
- No data found on how many ideas an individual associate screens per week, which would be the cleanest throughput benchmark.

## Q4. Role of expert networks (GLG, Tegus, AlphaSights) and customer interviews; primary vs secondary research.

### Takeaway
Expert networks are a mid-market/growth-stage and thesis-building tool, not a seed-stage one — most small VC funds run **0–5 expert sessions a month** at roughly **$950–$1,150 per hour** — while the conviction at pre-seed/seed comes overwhelmingly from *primary* calls the investor sources themselves, especially **back-channel** references that the founder did not stage. Founder-side discovery methodology sets the parallel benchmark: 5–20 interviews before building (The Mom Test), 100+ in ten weeks (Blank).

### Cited Findings
- **Usage intensity.** "Most sub-$200M VC funds run 0–5 expert network sessions per month." — [ValueAdd VC, "Expert Networks: $500–$2K/Hour, Worth It?"](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/expert-networks-for-investors-how-they-work-and-which-are-worth-paying-for)
- **Price.** Industry average expert fee "$950 per hour, rising to $1,150 for premium experts"; GLG projects reported at "$750–1500/hour"; AlphaSights typically "5–10% lower than GLG for comparable engagements." — [IQ Network, "Best Expert Networks in 2026"](https://iqnetwork.co/best-expert-networks/); [28 Experts, "GLG vs AlphaSights vs Guidepoint"](https://www.28experts.com/blog/how-much-are-you-really-paying-for-with-glg-alphasights-and-guidepoint)
- **Scale of the transcript corpus (the secondary-research substitute).** Tegus's Expert Transcript Library holds "over 300,000 transcripts covering 31,000+ public and private companies"; Tegus arranges "more than 900 expert calls each month" (an earlier release cited ~3,500/month). Note: Tegus is now part of AlphaSense, and its expert-call product is marketed as "Expert Calls On Demand with AlphaSense." — [AlphaSense Help Center, Tegus Expert Transcript Library](https://help.alpha-sense.com/hc/en-us/articles/43616741825939-Tegus-Expert-Transcript-Library-Understanding-Transcript-Types-Perspectives); [Tegus/AlphaSense Expert Calls](https://tegus.com/expert-calls); [Inex One directory entry](https://inex.one/expert-network-directory/tegus-expert-network)
- **Where conviction actually comes from.** "Back-channel customer and ex-coworker calls are where partners build real conviction, rather than the staged calls founders prepare for." Reference calls come in three types with different weight; "the forward customer reference is the call founders set up, which partners know is staged and therefore calibrate with discount." — [NextView Ventures](https://nextview.vc/blog/vcs-doing-the-work-to-build-investment-conviction/); [hub.causo.ai, "VC reference calls: what investors really ask (2026)"](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/vc-reference-calls)
- **Volume of reference calls.** "Lead investors typically run 10 to 20 reference calls before a term sheet, split across customers, coworkers, and peer investors." — [hub.causo.ai (2026)](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/vc-reference-calls)
- **Dedicated VC guidance on running customer reference calls** exists as a named practitioner artifact. — [Eric Rosenblum, Foothill Ventures, "Conducting customer reference calls: a perspective from a VC point of view"](https://medium.com/foothill-ventures/conducting-customer-reference-calls-a-perspective-from-a-vc-point-of-view-that-may-also-be-1f3799f50b3c)
- **Thesis-stage primary research volume.** Alpaca's Field Study involves speaking with "dozens of industry players, customers, and startups" over 3–4 months. — [Alpaca VC](https://medium.com/alpaca-vc/alpaca-vcs-field-study-program-a-repeatable-thesis-driven-approach-to-investment-5b1096108ff1)
- **High Alpha's exploration phase** pairs desk research ("markets, problems, trends, competitors, and new technologies") with "focused bursts of customer and expert interviews to validate assumptions" — i.e. secondary sets the hypothesis, primary tests it. — [High Alpha, "Unveiling the New Ventures Team's Approach"](https://www.highalpha.com/blog/new-ventures-team-approach-to-scaling-businesses)
- **Founder-side interview benchmarks.** The Mom Test was written for "founders doing customer discovery — typically 5–20 interviews before building"; a commonly circulated decision rule is that after 15–20 interviews you focus the MVP on "the single most painful problem mentioned by 60% or more of interviewees." *(The 60% threshold appears in secondary write-ups, not in Fitzpatrick's book as far as I could verify.)* — [Koji, "The Mom Test for Customer Interviews (2026)"](https://www.koji.so/blog/mom-test-customer-interviews-2026); [Koji, Mom Test methodology](https://www.koji.so/docs/mom-test-methodology)
- **The Mom Test's core rule**, in Fitzpatrick's words: "it's your job as the interviewer to ask questions so good that even the people who love you most can't lie to you" — i.e. ask about the interviewee's past behaviour and life, never about your idea. — [UXtweak, "What Is the Mom Test?"](https://blog.uxtweak.com/the-mom-test/)
- **Blank's volume benchmark.** In Blank's classes, students "speak to over 100 to 150 customers in ten weeks while taking a full course load"; during COVID he argued that without travel, teams "ought to be able to do more than 100 interviews." Blank's test of seriousness: "if you're using the word 'pivot' but haven't left your desk or made eye contact with potential customers, you haven't truly implemented 'get out of the building.'" — [Steve Blank, "Customer Discovery In the Time Of the Covid-19 Virus" (Apr 2020)](https://steveblank.com/2020/04/07/customer-discovery-in-the-time-of-the-covid-19-virus/)
- **Studio position on primary research.** Hexa: "people will take your solution if they need it and if they perceive that what you offer is significantly better than what they have today; rather than asking people if they like your solution, you need to understand what they really need"; and "great validation doesn't come from proving your assumptions, but comes from challenging them." — [Hexa, "How to validate a startup idea?"](https://www.hexa.com/blog/news/how-to-validate-a-startup-idea); [Thibaud Elzière, Inside Hexa](https://medium.com/inside-hexa/how-to-validate-a-startup-idea-6c0b031c8338)
- PSL frames voice-of-customer as the deciding mechanism: customer input "align[s] ideas with their ICP, value proposition, key features, and business model, giving them the confidence to kill or validate a business idea based on real customer input." — [PSL, "Voice of Customer: Your Compass in Startup Decision Making"](https://www.psl.com/feed-posts/voice-of-customer-your-compass-in-startup-decision-making)

### Inferences
- The primary/secondary split is stage-dependent and the sources support a rough shape rather than a hard number: **secondary research generates and frames the hypothesis; primary research is what changes the decision.** No source I found quantifies "X% of conviction comes from primary" — but every practitioner source locates the decisive evidence in calls (back-channel references, customer interviews, expert calls), and none locates it in desk work. The writer should say this qualitatively and flag the absence of a number.
- The unit economics of expert networks (~$1,000/hour, 0–5 calls/month at a small fund) mean a seed fund's *entire* annual expert-network budget is on the order of a few tens of thousands of dollars. An AI idea team cannot assume expert-network access as a baseline; the human benchmark at idea stage is closer to *free* primary calls the team sources via network, cold outreach and communities.
- The single most transferable quality standard is The Mom Test's falsification posture, which is identical to Hexa's "challenge, don't prove" and to Blank's "get out of the building": an AI team's interview synthesis should be scored on whether it captured *past behaviour and existing spend* rather than *stated interest in the idea.* Stated-interest evidence should be treated as near-zero weight.
- A defensible primary-evidence bar for an idea-stage memo, triangulating Mom Test (5–20) and lead-VC reference practice (10–20): **~10–20 conversations with named, role-specific people**, of which a meaningful share are not sourced by the person advocating the idea.

### Gaps
- **No source quantifies the primary/secondary conviction split.** I found no study or practitioner statement giving a percentage. This is a genuine gap, not an omission.
- I could not verify the "60% of interviewees" MVP-focusing rule against Fitzpatrick's primary text.
- I could not fetch the ValueAdd VC piece directly (egress blocked), so the "0–5 sessions per month for sub-$200M funds" figure rests on search-engine retrieval of that page.

## Q5. How do venture studios / corporate venture teams run idea validation differently from VCs? Documented process and hit rate.

### Takeaway
Studios differ structurally: they generate ideas internally, run a **fixed-stage funnel with an explicit kill gate** (PSL: ideation → validation → creation → spin-out → scale-up; High Alpha: Exploration → Sprint Week → form-and-fund), and they kill **~90%** of what they start. PSL's published numbers — 25 ideas worked in year one with 3 spun out; 250 ideas to 25 companies by 2020 — are the best-documented hit rate available. GSSN's oft-quoted outperformance statistics (84% raise seed, 72% reach Series A, 53% IRR, 25.2 months to Series A) are from a **2022** report and are self-interested; they should be quoted with heavy caveats.

### Cited Findings
- **PSL's five stages:** "ideation, validation, creation, spin out, scale up." At validation PSL tests "customer demand, technical feasibility, business model, unit economics, market dynamics, investor interest, and more," and "most ideas are killed"; if an idea survives validation, PSL considers it "a venture scale opportunity." — [InnoLead, Venture Studio Profile: Pioneer Square Labs](https://www.innovationleader.com/uncategorized/venture-studio-profile-pioneer-square-labs/); [PSL Studio](https://www.psl.com/studio)
- **PSL time budget and methods:** "roughly four months working hard to quickly validate" — prototypes, customer conversations, market analysis; PSL "leverages digital marketing to validate customer demand before they begin real work on any of their ideas." — [PSL Studio](https://www.psl.com/studio)
- **PSL hit rate, year one (2016):** worked on **25 separate startup ideas, killing all but three**. — [GeekWire, "How this 'startup studio' quickly axed 22 ideas and spun out three companies in one year"](https://www.geekwire.com/2016/startup-studio-quickly-axed-22-ideas-spun-three-companies-one-year/)

### Inferences
- The structural difference from VC is that a studio's research is **generative and adversarial toward its own ideas**, whereas a VC's is **confirmatory toward a founder's idea**. The studio must produce the hypothesis, the falsification test *and* the verdict, which is exactly the AI idea-team's position — so studios, not VCs, are the correct benchmark for the reader's system.
- **The headline benchmark number is the kill ratio: ~90%.** PSL's 250→25 is the best-documented; a studio-quality AI pipeline should expect to kill roughly 9 of 10 ideas it seriously investigates, and its process should be designed to do that cheaply and early rather than after a build.
- The GSSN statistics suffer from severe survivorship and selection bias: studios choose which ideas to spin out (so a "studio startup" is already a post-kill survivor), and GSSN is a studio trade body. The honest comparison is **250 ideas → 25 companies → N successes**, not "studio spin-outs beat startups." The report-writer should present GSSN numbers with this framing or they will mislead.
- Hexa's Opportunity Memo is the single most directly copyable artifact spec found in this research: it is a studio's pre-founder, pre-build document, its ten sections map cleanly onto machine-generable sections, and it names the standard for each (e.g. 3–5 concrete use cases; ICP defined by *buying centre* not just company type; sizing by top-down/bottom-up confrontation).
- High Alpha's Sprint Week is the clearest time-box in the literature: one week, cross-functional, ending in a binary form/don't-form decision, with live customer validation inside the week. That is the right shape for an AI team's "deep dive" tier.

### Gaps
- **Rocket Internet:** no current (2026) documented validation methodology found; Rocket's historical model was execution-speed cloning of proven Western models rather than idea validation, and I found no source in this research to cite for it. The writer should either drop Rocket or research it separately.
- **Antler:** no published, formal kill-criteria document found — only programme descriptions. Antler's cohort funding rate ("a handful of teams per cohort") is qualitative; I found no reliable percentage.
- **Atomic and Pioneer Square Labs** publish no current per-idea validation checklist; PSL's four-month figure and five-stage list are the extent of the public detail.
- No independent (non-industry-funded) study of venture studio returns was located; the CCSE paper was found but not read.

## Q6. What are the standard kill criteria / falsification tests used to reject an idea quickly?

### Takeaway
The modern convention is to name the **riskiest assumption** (RAT), pre-register a **PASS/FAIL threshold before running the test**, and climb a **validation ladder** from cheapest to most expensive evidence — interviews → smoke test → concierge/Wizard-of-Oz → paid pilot — stopping at the first rung that falsifies. The posture is explicitly Popperian: the job is to try to kill the idea.

### Cited Findings
- **RAT definition and budget.** "The Riskiest Assumption Test (RAT) designs a ≤2-week, ≤$100 behavioral experiment to validate the single assumption most likely to kill the idea." "Define your riskiest assumption — what is the single biggest belief that, if wrong, would kill your entire business?" — [Ainna, "Startup Idea Validation: The Nine-Dimension Assessment"](https://ainna.ai/resources/faq/startup-idea-validation-faq)
- **Assumption triage.** "Not all assumptions are equal. Some, if wrong, kill the business. Others are inconvenient but survivable. Your job is to rank them and test the riskiest ones first." — [Ainna](https://ainna.ai/resources/faq/startup-idea-validation-faq)
- **The falsification posture.** "The mental shift you need is from confirmation-seeking to falsifiability. Your job in validation is to try to kill your idea. If it survives your best attempts to break it, you have something worth building." — [Startup Ignition, "Startup Validation: The Complete Step-by-Step Guide (2026)"](https://www.startupignition.com/news/startup-validation-complete-guide/)
- **The validation ladder + pre-registration.** "Identify the RAT (the assumption that kills the business if wrong). Plan the validation ladder: interviews → smoke test → concierge/WoZ → paid pilot. Run the cheapest falsifiable test first; **pre-register PASS/FAIL thresholds and stop rules.**" — [Startup Ignition](https://www.startupignition.com/news/startup-validation-complete-guide/); [Startup Fundraising, "Test Your Startup Idea: A Guide to Validation & De-Risking"](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/how-to-test-your-business-idea)
- **Decision-memo output shape.** A validation decision memo should cover "verdict, strengths, risks, RAT experiment, kill criteria, and your next step." — [Ainna](https://ainna.ai/resources/faq/startup-idea-validation-faq)
- **Smoke-test thresholds (the quantitative kill gate).** Collect "at least 100–200 clicks before judging performance"; B2B SaaS landing-page benchmarks: self-serve pages 4–10%, **demo-request pages 1.5–4%**, content pages 0.5–2%; self-serve free trials ~8% median; email campaigns 5–20%+; organic search ~2.7%. Caveat from the same sources: "if your traffic doesn't reflect your ideal customer, your conversion data won't be meaningful," and for high-ticket niche B2B "even a 2% conversion could be significant if the leads are high quality." — [SaaSHero, B2B SaaS Conversion Rate Benchmarks 2026](https://www.saashero.net/strategy/b2b-saas-conversion-rate-benchmarks/); [Founder FAQs, "How to Run a Smoke Test Landing Page to Prove Demand"](https://founderfaqs.com/blogs/how-to-run-a-smoke-test-landing-page-to-prove-demand); [Do What Matter, "Fake door test: validate demand for a B2B SaaS offer"](https://dowhatmatter.com/guides/fake-door-test); [Powered by Search, B2B SaaS Landing Page Statistics](https://www.poweredbysearch.com/learn/b2b-saas-landing-page-stats/)
- **PSL's dimension-level kill list** (effectively the criteria set): customer demand, technical feasibility, business model, unit economics, market dynamics, investor interest. Failure on any one is grounds to kill. — [InnoLead / PSL](https://www.innovationleader.com/uncategorized/venture-studio-profile-pioneer-square-labs/)
- **Demand-signal-over-polish as the Antler kill standard:** teams "build the smallest artifact that produces evidence"; coaches "push for demand signal, not polish." — [SuperScout, Antler guide](https://superscout.co/investor/antler)
- **The substitution-cost criterion (Hexa).** An idea fails unless the buyer both *needs* it and *perceives it as significantly better than the status quo* — enough to change an existing habit. Liking the solution is not evidence. — [Hexa](https://www.hexa.com/blog/news/how-to-validate-a-startup-idea)
- **The anti-pattern to guard against.** "Fake validation" — evidence gathered in a way that can only confirm. — [Richin Jose, "Why 'Fake Validation' is Killing Startups"](https://richinjose.medium.com/why-fake-validation-is-killing-startups-15b1c9f539fc)
- **Evidence that this is already being encoded into AI agents** (directly relevant as prior art for the reader): an open-source agent team "from startup idea brainstorming to full validation and go-to-market strategy... for developers who'd rather validate in 10 minutes than regret in six months," built on Claude Code / Codex / Cursor. — [GitHub: MaxKmet/idea-validation-agents](https://github.com/MaxKmet/idea-validation-agents); a packaged startup-idea-validation skill: [LobeHub skills marketplace](https://lobehub.com/skills/vasilyu1983-ai-agents-public-startup-idea-validation)

### Inferences
- A concrete kill-criteria set for a EUR 1M-ARR software/AI/API idea, assembled from the above and stated as falsifiable tests with pre-registered thresholds:
  1. **Enumerability.** Can the ICP be enumerated from a real source, and does (accounts × plausible ACV × plausible win-rate) clear EUR 1M ARR without a capture-rate hand-wave? If the arithmetic only works at an unexplained 1% share — kill.
  2. **Existing spend.** Do interviewees already pay money or dedicated headcount for the status quo? Mom Test standard: past behaviour, not stated interest. No existing budget line — kill or reclassify as an education-heavy bet.
  3. **Switching delta (Hexa).** Is the offer *significantly* better, not marginally, relative to the incumbent habit? Marginal — kill.
  4. **Demand signal (smoke test).** Pre-register the threshold before running: ≥100–200 ICP-qualified clicks, demo-request conversion judged against the 1.5–4% B2B band, with traffic quality checked first. Below band with clean traffic — kill.
  5. **Competitive teardown.** Does a live competitor's pricing page already serve this at a fraction of the assumed ACV, or does an incumbent's roadmap make it a feature? — kill or re-position.
  6. **Defensibility (Hexa memo section).** Is there any accumulating advantage after 24 months? None — kill for venture scale (may still be a business).
  7. **Feasibility.** Especially for AI/API businesses: does it require a model capability that does not yet reliably exist? — defer, don't kill.
  8. **Founder-fit (Hexa "Founder Requirements").** Can the required hard/soft skills actually be staffed? Not staffable — kill regardless of merit.
- The single most important process convention to copy is **pre-registration of PASS/FAIL thresholds before the test is run.** This is the only structural defence against an AI system (or a human) rationalising an ambiguous result into a pass, and it is the mechanism that turns "research" into "diligence."
- The RAT budget (≤2 weeks, ≤$100) is a useful upper bound for an idea-stage falsification test and is roughly two orders of magnitude cheaper than a single expert-network call.

### Gaps
- Most kill-criteria material found is from methodology/content sites rather than from named studios. No studio publishes its actual kill-gate scorecard with thresholds; PSL, High Alpha, Atomic and Antler all describe *dimensions* but not *thresholds*.
- No source gave documented, studio-internal smoke-test conversion thresholds (e.g. "PSL kills below X% signup"). The conversion benchmarks cited are general B2B SaaS marketing benchmarks being repurposed as validation gates — that repurposing is mine and should be presented as an inference, not as studio practice.
- I found no 2026-dated study measuring whether pre-registered kill criteria improve venture outcomes.
